A South Carolina mother of three who vanished in 2020 has been found dead, and her husband has now been arrested in connection with her death, bringing a grim and long-delayed turn to a missing-person case that haunted investigators for years.

Adrean Capers was 31 years old when she was reported missing to the North Charleston Police Department in March 2020. She was a mother of three, and for years the central question in her case was painfully simple: where had she gone? Like many missing-person investigations, the early search depended on last known movements, witness accounts, records, and the hope that someone, somewhere, had seen something that could explain what happened.

Authorities later said Adrean was last seen at an Econo Lodge in Summerville, South Carolina. That detail became one of the most important anchors in the case. A hotel sighting can help investigators build a timeline, but it can also leave painful gaps. A person can be seen in one place, then disappear from public view, and the hours or days that follow may remain unclear unless cameras, phone records, financial activity, or witnesses fill in the missing pieces.

For years, Adrean's disappearance remained unresolved. Missing-person cases often become harder as time passes. Memories fade, people move, records become harder to retrieve, and physical evidence can be lost or degraded. Families are left waiting for answers while investigators continue to revisit old leads and look for new ways to test what they already know.

The case changed in 2025. On May 9, 2025, authorities determined that Adrean most likely went missing from the Econo Lodge at 110 Holiday Drive in Summerville, but was likely killed in Charleston County. That conclusion shifted the investigation. The question was no longer only where Adrean had gone. It became where she had died, who may have been involved, and whether evidence could still be found after so many years.

The Charleston County Sheriff's Office took over the case. Then, on June 11, 2026, detectives executed a search warrant on property belonging to Adrean's husband's father in the 7400 block of Carnes Road in Hollywood, South Carolina. During that search, investigators found remains inside a septic tank on the property, according to authorities.

The discovery was shocking, not only because of where the remains were found, but because of how long Adrean had been missing. A septic tank is not a place searchers would normally examine unless investigators had a specific reason to look there. The search suggested that detectives had developed information pointing them toward that property and toward a very specific location on it.

After the remains were found, the Charleston County Coroner's Office examined the body. Authorities said dental records and still-intact tattoos helped confirm that the remains were Adrean's. Identification in cases involving decomposed remains can be difficult, and investigators often rely on dental records, fingerprints when available, DNA, medical records, tattoos, or other distinguishing features. In Adrean's case, officials said dental records and tattoos were important in confirming her identity.

For Adrean's family, the identification would have ended one kind of uncertainty while opening another. After years of not knowing where she was, they learned that she had been found. But the discovery also raised new questions about how she died, when she died, how her remains ended up in a septic tank, and why the truth had remained hidden for so long.

Authorities later tracked Adrean's husband, Harry Capers III, to Gwinnett County, Georgia. Officials said he had most recently been living there after moving to Texas, California, and Florida in the six years since Adrean vanished. Movement across multiple states can complicate an investigation, especially when investigators are trying to locate a person, serve warrants, coordinate with other agencies, and determine where a suspect can be taken into custody.

Harry Capers III, 42, of Hollywood, South Carolina, was arrested on charges related to fraud and theft, according to authorities. He was extradited on Monday, November 23, to Charleston County, where he was booked into the Sheriff Al Cannon Detention Center.

The Charleston County Sheriff's Office announced his arrest in connection with Adrean's death and disappearance. He is charged with murder, desecration of human remains, and possession of a weapon during a violent crime, according to the sheriff's office statement. Those are serious charges, but they are allegations. He is accused, not convicted, and the case will now move through the criminal justice system.

In addition to the Charleston County charges, authorities said the Berkeley County Sheriff's Office has fraud warrants out against him, and the Summerville Police Department has a kidnapping warrant out against him. The existence of multiple warrants in different jurisdictions can make a case more complex, because prosecutors and investigators may need to coordinate evidence, custody, court appearances, and the order in which charges are handled.

The murder charge is the most serious allegation. In general, a murder charge means prosecutors allege that a person unlawfully killed another person with the required criminal intent under state law. The desecration charge relates to the alleged treatment of human remains after death. The weapon charge alleges possession of a weapon during a violent crime. Each charge will require proof, and each will be tested through court proceedings, evidence, and the defense's response.

At this stage, many details remain unknown to the public. Authorities have not, in the information released so far, explained every step that led them to the property on Carnes Road. They have not publicly detailed all of the evidence that connects the accused to Adrean's death. They also have not answered every question about the timeline between Adrean's last known sighting and the discovery of her remains.

Those unanswered questions are likely to be central as the case moves forward. Investigators and prosecutors will need to establish what happened, when it happened, and how the evidence supports the charges. The defense will have the opportunity to challenge the evidence, question the investigation, and argue that the state has not proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt.

Cases like this draw intense public attention because they combine several fears at once: a missing mother, a long silence, a hidden body, and an arrest years later. People often ask how someone can disappear for so long without being found. The answer is rarely simple. Rural properties can be large. Private land can be difficult to search without legal authority. Remains can be concealed in places that are not obvious. And investigators usually need probable cause, warrants, tips, records, or forensic leads before they can search specific locations.

The septic tank discovery also shows how missing-person cases can change when investigators develop a new lead. A case may appear cold for years, then suddenly move because a record is re-examined, a witness speaks, a property becomes relevant, or a forensic detail points in a new direction. What looks like a sudden breakthrough may actually be the result of long, quiet work behind the scenes.

For now, the confirmed facts are stark. Adrean Capers, a 31-year-old mother of three, was reported missing in March 2020. She was last seen at an Econo Lodge in Summerville. Years later, authorities determined she had likely been killed in Charleston County. In June 2026, investigators searched property in Hollywood and found remains in a septic tank. Dental records and tattoos helped identify those remains as Adrean's. In November 2026, her husband was arrested and charged in connection with her death.

The case is not finished. An arrest is not a conviction, and a charge is not proof. But after six years, Adrean Capers has been found, and the investigation has moved from a missing-person mystery to a criminal case. The next chapter will be decided in court, where the evidence will be tested and where the central question will no longer be where Adrean went, but what the state can prove about how she died.
