The knock came on a Saturday morning, while I was boxing up Grandma's teacups.

A man in a gray windbreaker stood on the porch with a folder under his arm. He smiled like we had already met. "You must be the cleaner," he said. "I'm just here to measure the kitchen before closing."

I set the box down very slowly. "Closing on what?"

## The house we both inherited

Grandma Ruth raised my sister Dana and me in that house for most of our childhood. Our mom worked nights, so the little blue house on Alder Street was where we did homework, scraped our knees and learned to make pie crust that never quite came out right.

When Grandma died in the spring, her will was short and clear. The house went to both of us, fifty-fifty. Her letter, tucked inside the will, said she hoped we would "decide together, and kindly."

Dana lives three states away. I live twenty minutes from Alder Street. So I was the one who cleared the gutters, paid the water bill and spent my weekends sorting forty years of Grandma's life into boxes. Dana called once a month. She always said the same thing: "No rush. We'll figure it out."

## A contract I had never seen

The man on the porch was polite about it. He showed me a purchase agreement for the house. The price was well below what similar homes on the street had sold for. At the bottom was one signature: Dana's.

"She said she was handling everything for the family," he told me, suddenly less sure of himself. "She said her sibling had agreed."

I hadn't agreed to anything. I hadn't even been asked.

I asked him to leave, politely, and then I sat on the floor of Grandma's kitchen for a long time, holding a teacup with a chipped rose on the handle.

## The phone call

Dana picked up on the second ring. I didn't yell. I had promised myself I wouldn't.

"Someone came to measure the kitchen today," I said. "For the closing."

There was a long silence. Then she started talking very fast. She was behind on her mortgage. Her husband's hours had been cut. The buyer was a friend of a friend who could close in thirty days, cash. She was going to tell me. She was going to split the money. She just needed it to happen quickly, before I "got sentimental" and slowed everything down.

"You signed a contract for a house you only half own," I said.

"I thought you'd say no," she admitted. "You always say no to selling."

That part stung, because it was true. I had said, more than once, that I wasn't ready.

## What I found out

The next Monday, I called a real estate attorney. I won't pretend I understood all of it, but the basics were simple: when two people own a house together, one of them can't sell the whole thing alone. The contract Dana signed couldn't transfer my half. The buyer, who it turned out had not been told the full story either, backed out within a week.

I could have stopped there. Part of me wanted to. Part of me wanted Dana to feel as blindsided as I had felt on that porch.

Instead, I opened Grandma's letter again. *Decide together, and kindly.*

## The compromise

I flew out to see Dana a month later. We sat at her kitchen table, which was covered in bills she hadn't wanted me to see.

We talked for six hours. She cried. I cried. She apologized for going behind my back, and I apologized for treating "not ready" like a final answer when she was drowning.

Here is what we agreed:

- We would get a proper appraisal from someone neither of us knew.
- I would buy out Dana's half at that price, with a loan from my credit union.
- If I couldn't get the loan in ninety days, we would list the house together, with one agent, and split everything evenly.

The loan came through on day seventy-one.

## Where we are now

I live in the little blue house on Alder Street now. The kitchen still smells faintly of cinnamon, even though nobody has baked a proper pie in it for a year.

Dana caught up on her mortgage. Things between us are not perfect. There are still moments when I remember the man on the porch and feel the floor tilt a little. But she visited last Thanksgiving, and we tried Grandma's pie crust recipe together.

It still didn't come out right. We laughed until we couldn't breathe.
